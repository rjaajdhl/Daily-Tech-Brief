Write one complete Daily Tech Brief article about the event described in EVENT and SOURCES.

The article must be factually precise, technically detailed, and natural to read from beginning to end. It should read like a well-edited technical news article, not a source-by-source summary, research-note reconstruction, press-release rewrite, or translated report.

AUDIENCE

The reader is technically experienced across AI/ML, software, cloud and infrastructure, networking, and cybersecurity.

Assume familiarity with common industry terminology. Do not stop to define widely used concepts, technologies, protocols, attack classes, software components, or development terminology.

Explain only names, acronyms, internal systems, specialized standards, benchmarks, legal terms, or event-specific concepts that would reasonably be unfamiliar even to an experienced technical reader.

Do not simplify away technical detail merely to make the article easier to read. Improve the order and presentation of the information instead.

CORE EDITORIAL PRINCIPLES

1. Understand the event before deciding the structure.

Do not follow the order of SOURCES, EVENT, or GENRE mechanically.

Read the available material as a whole, determine what actually happened, what mechanism or conditions matter, what was demonstrated, what remains uncertain, and what changed afterward.

Then organize the article in the order that makes the event easiest to understand.

There is no fixed article sequence.

A reproduction result may come before the root cause if that makes the issue immediately understandable.
A mechanism may come first if the examples make little sense without it.
A response may need to appear early if it materially changes how the event should be interpreted.

The source's structure is not the article's structure.


2. Establish a clear center of gravity.

Each article should have one primary event, finding, release, vulnerability, incident, policy change, or development at its center.

The headline, lead, and body should all revolve around that same center.

Do not give every available fact equal weight.

Prioritize the facts needed to understand:
- what happened,
- how it worked,
- under what conditions,
- what was actually demonstrated,
- what the impact was,
- how the relevant parties responded,
- and what important limitations remain.

Preserve useful detail. Do not remove technically meaningful material simply because it is secondary to the headline.


3. Do not optimize for brevity.

The article may be long when the event is complex and the sources contain useful detail.

Preserve important:
- technical mechanisms,
- reproduction steps,
- conditions,
- environment details,
- measurements,
- timelines,
- concrete examples,
- comparisons,
- stakeholder positions,
- limitations,
- remediation,
- and response details.

Remove repetition, not substance.

The goal is not a short article. The goal is a dense article that remains easy to follow.


4. Let strong facts carry the article.

Do not habitually add interpretation after facts that are already self-explanatory.

Avoid editorial filler such as:
- "This highlights..."
- "This demonstrates..."
- "The key takeaway is..."
- "What makes this important is..."
- "This suggests..."
- "The common thread is..."
- "This is significant because..."

unless the source itself makes that interpretation and the attribution matters.

If a model submitted a real police tip, a server command actually executed, a live SIM redirected a call, or a vendor responded within 48 minutes, describe the fact accurately and let it stand.


5. Explain technical mechanisms in the order a technical reader would understand them.

Do not begin with a wall of implementation names when the mechanism itself has not yet been established.

When useful, first explain what happens at the system level, then introduce exact implementation details such as:
- permissions,
- packages,
- processes,
- APIs,
- intents,
- functions,
- headers,
- protocol fields,
- configuration keys,
- or component names.

Do not remove the implementation detail. Present it after the reader has enough context to understand why it matters.

Likewise, do not over-explain familiar technical concepts.


6. Keep separate issues separate.

If the source material contains multiple vulnerabilities, mechanisms, incidents, follow-up findings, or platform behaviors with different causes or conditions, do not merge them into a single issue.

Make their relationship clear when each first appears.

A third-party application flaw and a separate platform-level flaw should not read as one vulnerability merely because they appear in the same disclosure.

Use transitions such as "Separately" or "In a different path" only when they genuinely clarify the relationship. Do not turn them into repetitive structural labels.


7. Do not turn examples into an inventory.

Avoid mechanical structures such as:

Example A...
Example B...
Example C...
Company response...
Mitigation...

when the cases belong to a common mechanism and can be explained more naturally together.

At the same time, give important examples enough space when their details materially improve understanding.

Each paragraph should add a new piece of understanding rather than simply represent the next item in the source material.


8. Preserve details that make the event concrete.

Do not discard useful details solely because they appear anecdotal.

Preserve details when they help the reader understand what actually occurred, such as:
- an exact action taken by a model,
- a specific phrase it generated,
- how a person discovered the incident,
- a live-system reproduction result,
- the time between action and effect,
- an unexpected bypass path,
- a third party independently noticing the behavior,
- or how quickly a vendor responded.

Use judgment. Details should support the event rather than decorate the prose.


HEADLINE

Write a concrete headline that communicates what actually happened.

Do not default to formulas such as:
- "Company X announces..."
- "Researcher Y reveals..."
- "Vendor Z discloses..."

when the actual behavior, vulnerability, incident, or technical result is more informative.

The subject may still be a company or researcher when that is genuinely the clearest construction.

The headline should tell the reader what happened and, where useful, the important consequence, condition, or response.

Do not make the headline stronger than the sources support.
Do not use clickbait.
Do not turn a possibility into a demonstrated exploit or a reported claim into a confirmed incident.


LEAD

The lead should give the reader a map of the article, not merely repeat the first body paragraph in shorter form.

It should naturally establish the most important combination of:
- what happened,
- the central mechanism or condition,
- the demonstrated result or practical impact,
- the most important response,
- and any verification limitation that materially changes how the event should be read.

Do not force every item into the lead.

However, do not hide a major structural fact until the middle of the body.

If the article contains two distinct vulnerabilities, the lead should not make them appear to be one.
If the entire incident is based on one person's account and has not been independently verified, make that clear in the lead.
If a result was demonstrated only in an emulator and not on a physical system, mention that early when it changes the interpretation.


BODY

Write multiple natural paragraphs separated by blank lines.

Do not use section headings, bold labels, or structural labels such as:
- Background
- Details
- Cause
- Exploit
- Impact
- Mitigation
- Response
- Technical Analysis

Let paragraph transitions carry the structure.

Use the concrete facts in SOURCES densely when they are relevant:
- sequence,
- timing,
- numbers,
- system configuration,
- prerequisites,
- architecture,
- exploit path,
- model behavior,
- reproduction,
- comparisons,
- stakeholder positions,
- vendor/platform decisions,
- remediation,
- and limitations.

A paragraph should have a coherent purpose, but do not mechanically assign one paragraph to every source item or example.

Do not repeat the same point in the lead and multiple body paragraphs unless additional detail materially advances the story.


PROSE AND SENTENCE FLOW

Write natural professional English.

Avoid repetitive sentence openings with the same company, researcher, publication, or institution.

Do not produce a sequence such as:

"Anthropic said..."
"Anthropic said..."
"Anthropic said..."

or:

"Saini found..."
"Saini found..."
"Saini found..."

when the event, model, test, mechanism, component, response, or result would be a more natural grammatical subject.

Do not solve repetition by replacing names mechanically with:
- "the company,"
- "the researcher,"
- "the vendor,"

in every following sentence.

Do not solve repetition by converting everything into passive voice.

Vary sentence structure naturally while preserving clear attribution whenever the identity of the speaker, evaluator, reporter, or decision-maker matters.

Avoid prose that sounds like a translation, compliance report, or generated research summary.


ATTRIBUTION

Use primary sources as the preferred basis for technical facts when available.

Attribute facts to a publication when they come only from that publication.

Explicit attribution is especially important for:
- personal claims,
- disputed events,
- company self-assessments,
- unverified incidents,
- interpretations,
- future plans,
- statements from a single publication,
- or decisions by a specific vendor or platform.

Examples:
- "Mac said..."
- "Google classified the report as..."
- "According to Business Insider..."
- "Anthropic assessed the incidents as..."

Do not repeat the same attribution at the beginning of every paragraph when the source context remains clear.

Source tags provide evidence separately and should still be attached to factual sentences.


CLAIMS, VERIFICATION, AND EVIDENCE

Preserve the distinction between:
- allegation and confirmation,
- report and independent verification,
- planned change and completed deployment,
- possibility and demonstrated impact,
- emulator reproduction and physical-device reproduction,
- lab result and production behavior,
- vendor self-assessment and independent evaluation,
- candidate exposure and confirmed vulnerability,
- public availability and limited preview.

If an event is based primarily on a person's claim, write it as a claim.

If independent verification is important to the reader's interpretation, do not bury that fact at the end.

Conversely, if a behavior was actually reproduced and documented, do not weaken it into vague possibility language.


PRESERVE SOURCE STRENGTH

Do not strengthen or weaken claims beyond the source.

Preserve distinctions such as:
- may
- can
- could
- observed
- reproduced
- confirmed
- assessed
- believed
- planned
- deployed

Do not turn "could" into "does."
Do not turn "we believe" into an established fact.
Do not turn a lab reproduction into evidence of exploitation in the wild.

Do not weaken a confirmed reproduction into speculation when the source clearly demonstrates it.


TECHNICAL FIDELITY

Commands, configuration values, headers, code, function names, permissions, packages, protocol identifiers, and URLs must retain the original source spelling when used.

Wrap exact technical strings in backticks.

Do not translate, normalize, abbreviate, or rewrite them into semantically similar forms.

Do not add technical meaning that is not present in the source.

Numbers, dates, times, versions, quantities, prices, and units must come from SOURCES.

Preserve the meaning of time ranges and time zones.
Do not convert a time range into a single timestamp or otherwise invent precision.

Do not introduce numbers merely because they would make the article feel more complete.


LIMITATIONS

State a limitation when it materially changes the meaning of the result.

Examples include:
- emulator-only reproduction,
- no physical SIM validation,
- vendor-only benchmark,
- abstract-only paper,
- community claim without independent evidence,
- no vendor confirmation,
- limited preview rather than public release.

Describe the actual limitation directly.

Do not write meta statements about the input packet such as:
- "The provided sources do not include..."
- "The excerpt does not say..."
- "The available material is incomplete..."
- "There is not enough information in the supplied text..."

The reader does not know or care about the construction of the source packet.


PREVIOUSLY PUBLISHED

If PREVIOUSLY PUBLISHED is present, center the article on meaningful new facts.

Repeat earlier material only when necessary to understand the update.

Do not create labels such as:
- Previous coverage
- New information
- Update

Integrate the necessary context naturally.

If there are no meaningful new facts, skip the article.


GENRE GUIDE

The GENRE GUIDE is a coverage guide, not an article template.

It identifies facts that are commonly important for that type of event.

Do not copy its order into the article.

Choose the structure that best fits the actual event and available evidence.


GLOSSARY

The glossary is for event-specific terms that may be unfamiliar even to an experienced technical reader.

Appropriate entries may include:
- unusual internal project names,
- uncommon standards,
- specialized benchmarks,
- obscure regulatory names,
- uncommon acronyms,
- organization-specific terminology.

Do not add:
- common technical terminology,
- ordinary company or product names,
- concepts whose purpose is already obvious from the article.

Each definition should be one line.

Do not introduce new event facts, numbers, claims, or analysis in the glossary.

If nothing needs explanation, return [].


SOURCE TAGS

Every factual sentence in lead and body must end with one or more source tags such as [S1] or [S1][S3].

Use only sources that actually support the full claim in that sentence.

Names, numbers, dates, values, technical behavior, and quoted details must exist in SOURCES.

Do not fabricate evidence to complete the prose.


DO NOT

Do not:
- invent facts, motives, causal explanations, or implications,
- add generic lessons or takeaways,
- mechanically summarize SOURCES in their original order,
- use the GENRE GUIDE as a visible or invisible article outline,
- repeatedly begin paragraphs with the same company, researcher, or publication,
- define common technical terms like a textbook,
- overstate a headline,
- describe the internal source packet to the reader,
- add unsupported interpretation merely to make the article sound analytical,
- turn missing source material into speculation.


SKIP

Return skip only when:

1. PREVIOUSLY PUBLISHED already contains the event and SOURCES contain no meaningful new fact; or

2. the available source material contains only headline-level information and does not provide enough concrete facts to write a substantive article.

Do not skip because the event is difficult to explain or because the article would be long.

If skipping for insufficient material, state briefly what essential information is missing.
