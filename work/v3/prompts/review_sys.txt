Review the draft against SOURCE DIGEST.

The purpose of the review is to catch factual errors, material omissions, and editorial structure problems that make the event meaningfully harder to understand.

Do not rewrite the article yourself.

Numbers, exact code strings, exact timestamps, and source-tag existence are validated separately by deterministic checks. Focus on meaning, scope, attribution, conditions, sequence, technical mechanism, verification status, and editorial structure.

Use only facts contained in SOURCE DIGEST.
Do not infer facts from links, tables, attachments, or references that are mentioned but not actually present in the supplied text.
Do not request unsupported additions.

Return four lists:

blocking
missing
editorial
minor


BLOCKING

Use blocking only for factual or semantic errors that cannot remain in the article.

Examples:
- a statement contradicts the source,
- the article invents a fact,
- a weak or conditional source claim is written as confirmed fact,
- a claim is attributed to the wrong party,
- the scope, version, date, or condition is materially wrong,
- planned work is described as completed,
- private or limited availability is described as public,
- an emulator result is described as a physical-device result,
- two distinct events or vulnerabilities are incorrectly merged,
- an unrelated event is presented as part of the main incident.

Give a specific correction and cite the relevant wording or fact from SOURCE DIGEST.


MISSING

Use missing when an important fact from SOURCE DIGEST is absent and the omission materially weakens understanding of the event.

Examples:
- the core mechanism is missing,
- a prerequisite or exploit condition is missing,
- the actual reproduction result is missing,
- a critical response or mitigation is missing,
- the article omits the distinction between two separate issues,
- a major limitation is missing,
- the body mostly repeats the lead and fails to convey the concrete technical details available in SOURCE DIGEST.

Do not mark small details as missing.
Minor omissions belong in minor.


EDITORIAL

Use editorial only for structural problems that materially reduce readability or comprehension even though the underlying facts may be correct.

Examples:
- the article mechanically follows the order of the source material rather than the order needed to understand the event,
- the core mechanism appears so late that earlier paragraphs are difficult to interpret,
- two separate issues are not clearly distinguished until late in the article,
- the lead fails to establish a major fact needed to understand the body,
- repeated sentence openings with the same company, researcher, or publication make the article noticeably mechanical,
- implementation details are listed before the reader has enough context to understand what they do,
- facts are individually correct but arranged in a way that forces the reader to reread earlier paragraphs,
- the article reads primarily like a research-summary reconstruction rather than a coherent technical news article.

Do not use editorial for subjective preferences such as:
- making a sentence prettier,
- shortening an article,
- choosing a different synonym,
- minor rhythm improvements,
- personal stylistic taste.

An editorial issue should justify another drafting pass.


MINOR

Use minor for everything else that may be worth polishing but does not justify rewriting the article by itself.

Examples:
- isolated awkward phrasing,
- small wording improvements,
- unnecessary glossary entries,
- slight repetition,
- minor stylistic issues,
- a less-than-ideal but still clear headline.

Do not promote a minor preference into editorial merely to force another draft.
